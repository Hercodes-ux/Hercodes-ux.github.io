Why the naive answer is wrong

The two groups were never comparable

Users who adopted the feature were already the platform’s biggest fans. Their loyalty scores pile up at the high end; control users spread the other way. Comparing their watch time raw compares loyalty, not the feature.

Used the feature (n = 2,018) Control (n = 2,982)

Loyalty score (0–100), users per 10-point bin. Average loyalty: 65.72 treated vs 38.83 control.

The generator built this trap in on purpose — “superfan bias”: loyal users were 3× more likely to use the feature, and every loyalty point adds watch time on its own. Any raw comparison inherits that bias.

Same users, fairer comparison

Compare treated users to their twins, not to everyone

Each treated user was matched to a control “twin” with a near-identical loyalty score. The twins watch almost as much as the treated users — so almost all of the naive gap was loyalty, not the feature.

Method

Two subtractions — one of them is causal

Naive lift — biased

mean(treated watch) − mean(control watch)

44.91 − 29.47 = 15.44 hrs. Assumes the two groups were alike before the feature. They weren’t — treated users averaged 65.72 loyalty vs 38.83.

Causal lift (ATT) — matched

mean over pairs of (treated watch − twin watch)

Average of 2,018 within-pair differences = 1.9065 hrs. Each treated user is compared only with a control user of near-identical loyalty, so the loyalty effect cancels.

Why we know the truth here

Because the data is simulated, the generator’s formula is known — the one luxury real data never gives you. The estimate recovered 95.3% of it (error: −0.0935 hrs).

watch_time = 10 + 2.0 × used_feature + 0.5 × loyalty + noise
